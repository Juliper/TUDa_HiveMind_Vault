> [!NOTE] Definition
> The Hawthorne Effect is the tendency for study participants to modify their behavior, often performing better or more carefully than usual, simply because they are aware they are being observed as part of a study.

## Why It Matters

Because the Hawthorne Effect is itself an uncontrolled variable, it can act as a [[Control, Random, and Confounding Variables|confounding variable]]: an observed improvement might reflect increased attentiveness under observation rather than a genuine effect of the independent variable being tested.

## Mitigations

- Make observation as unobtrusive as possible (see the obtrusive/unobtrusive dimension in [[Controlled Experiment]])
- Include a baseline or control condition where participants are equally observed, so the observation effect is present in all conditions and cancels out in the comparison
- Allow a warm-up period so participants become accustomed to being observed before data collection begins

## Related Concepts

- [[Control, Random, and Confounding Variables]]: the Hawthorne Effect is a common source of confounds in field studies and lab experiments alike
- [[Validity and Reliability]]: an uncontrolled Hawthorne Effect threatens internal validity
- [[Controlled Experiment]]: obtrusiveness of the study format directly influences how strong this effect is
