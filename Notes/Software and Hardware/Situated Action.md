> [!NOTE] Definition
> Situated Action, introduced by Lucy Suchman (1987), argues that human action is fundamentally improvised and shaped moment-to-moment by the specific circumstances a person is in, rather than being the execution of a pre-existing, complete mental plan.

## Plans vs. Situated Action

Traditional cognitive models (such as the [[Model Human Processor]]) treat action as the execution of a plan formed in advance: a person decides a sequence of steps, then carries them out. Suchman's ethnographic studies of users struggling with a "smart" photocopier showed that people instead constantly improvise based on the resources and feedback available in the moment.

| View | Action Is... |
|---|---|
| **Planning model** | The execution of a predetermined, internally represented plan |
| **Situated Action** | A response improvised from the immediate situation, using plans only as a loose resource |

## Why It Matters for Design

If users do not follow rigid plans, interfaces should not assume they will proceed through a fixed sequence of steps. Instead, systems should:

- Provide rich, continuous feedback about the current state, since users constantly reassess their next move
- Support recovery and improvisation rather than forcing users down a single predicted path
- Be studied through observation of real use (see [[Ethnography in HCI]]) rather than only through abstract task models

## Example

Suchman observed users of an "intelligent" copier that tried to guide them through a fixed help sequence. Users did not follow the machine's assumed plan - they reacted to whatever the screen showed at each moment, often becoming confused because the system's model of "the plan" diverged from their situated, improvised behavior.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Situated Action does not claim plans are useless - it claims plans are resources for improvisation, not scripts that are executed step by step.
